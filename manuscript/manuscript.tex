\begin{abstract}
A weighted $k$-out-of-$n$:G system works when the total capacity of its working components reaches a demand
threshold. In early-stage design both the unit capacities and the demand are often expert estimates, yet the
weighted models with imprecise capacities that we identified keep the demand crisp and evaluate given designs. This paper models capacities and demand as triangular fuzzy numbers and compares them by a
Hurwicz rule on $\alpha$-cuts with a cut level and an optimism index. The rule contains the crisp model, the
interval worst and best cases and the necessity-, possibility- and credibility-based chance constraints, so
the attitude towards imprecision becomes an explicit design input. When all capacities share one proportional
spread, the two indices act on the design only through one integer threshold; a two-phase branch-and-bound
algorithm certifies the optimal design at each threshold, and a finite list of designs covers every index value.
On a generation fleet built from the IEEE Reliability Test System, seven fleets costing \$130--203\,M cover the
whole index range, two of which a $30$-cell grid over the indices never finds, and the crisp design fails the
reliability target on $6.8\%$ of the range and overpays on a further $32.1\%$.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Weighted $k$-out-of-$n$:G system, fuzzy reliability, redundancy allocation, possibilistic chance constraint, Hurwicz criterion, branch-and-bound, generation adequacy}

\section{Introduction}

A weighted $k$-out-of-$n$:G system (Wk/n:G) consists of $n$ independent components, component $i$
contributes an operational capacity $w_i>0$, and the system works if and only if the total capacity of the
working components reaches a threshold $k$:
\begin{equation*}
  \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i \, s_i \;\geq\; k, \quad s_i \in \{0, 1\},
\end{equation*}
where $s_i = 1$ when component $i$ works. The model generalizes the $k$-out-of-$n$:G system to components of
unequal performance, as in generation fleets, pumping stations and production lines with parallel units. Wu
and Chen\cite{WuChen1994} gave the $O(n\cdot k)$ recursion that underlies later evaluation methods\cite{Kuo2007}.
The model has since been extended to multi-state components and performance levels\cite{Zhuang2022,DingZuoLisnianski2010,Levitin2005},
three-state components\cite{Eryilmaz2019}, randomly weighted and heterogeneous
components\cite{ZHANG2021107184,EryilmazSarikaya2014}, the capacity lost upon system failure\cite{Eryilmaz2021},
dynamic and time-dependent settings of weighted and related $k$-out-of-$n$ systems\cite{EryilmazBozbulut2014,Xu2025}, several component types with optimization of the
design\cite{Ozkut2025}, and maintenance planning\cite{Sharifi2023corr,LorvandKelkinnama2023,Yang2022cbm,SinghSingh2025};
related $k$-out-of-$n$ structures continue to be generalized\cite{Gao2024sparse}.

Most of this literature treats the capacities $w_i$ as precisely known, and the threshold $k$ as a fixed
constant. In early-stage infrastructure design neither holds. Unit capacities change with wear and ambient
conditions and are often known through expert estimates, and the threshold is a design judgement derived from
regulations, demand forecasts or engineering consensus. Both carry epistemic uncertainty, which arises from
incomplete knowledge rather than from variability in repeated trials, and for which a probability distribution
adds an assumption that the data do not support\cite{Helton2004,Zio2009}; possibility theory represents such
knowledge without a distributional assumption\cite{InuiguchiRamik2000,DuboisPrade2012}. \new{To make the
consequences concrete, consider a regional grid operator that must deliver ``at least $180$\,MW'' at peak
demand. The figure is a regulator's target negotiated from demand projections, and the available output of
each unit depends on ambient temperature and wear.} A crisp formulation fixes both sides at nominal values and
thereby adopts a risk posture without stating it: a design feasible at nominal capacities may have zero
necessity of meeting demand over the plausible range of capacities, whereas requiring demand to be met with
high necessity may need considerably more redundancy and capital.

\textbf{Imprecise thresholds and capacities.} Several threshold models relax the crisp $k$, but each keeps
the threshold precise in some sense: multi-state models use several crisp values\cite{Zhuang2022},
phased-mission models a crisp value per phase\cite{WangXingAmari2020,WU2025110678}, and random-threshold
models a probability distribution that needs frequency data\cite{Oszczypala2024}.
On the component side, Ding et al.\cite{DingZuoLisnianski2010} represent the weights and state probabilities
of multi-state weighted systems by fuzzy numbers and evaluate adequacy against a prescribed demand; within
Liu's uncertainty theory, Gao et al.\cite{Gao2018} and Wei et al.\cite{WeiShiZhang2025a,WeiShiZhang2025b}
analyze weighted systems whose weights and states are uncertain variables, and Sheng and Ke\cite{ShengKe2020}
multi-state $k$-out-of-$n$ systems. In all of these the threshold is a prescribed constant, and no design problem is posed. Fuzzy
reliability expressions for weighted and consecutive structures\cite{ChaubeSingh2016,NegiSinghBisht2021,NegiSingh2019},
possibilistic reliability of $k$-out-of-$n$ systems\cite{HuangLiLiu2010} and fuzzy or possibilistic
allocation models\cite{Chebouba2020,Ashraf2019,ZhaoLiu2005,MANECKSHAW2026129956} likewise fuzzify the component
side while the failure boundary stays crisp; in a fuzzy-goal model of a multi-state weighted allocation the
reliability requirement and the budget are fuzzy, but capacities and threshold are crisp\cite{Ebrahimipur2011}.
Allocation models also fix one reading of the imprecise data in advance: a possibility\cite{LiuIwamura1998} or
credibility\cite{Liu2002,HuangJiangYeh2020} chance constraint, or a minimax-regret criterion over
intervals\cite{FeizollahiModarres2012}.

Once both sides of the success condition are fuzzy, the condition is no longer an inequality between numbers,
and a comparison rule must be chosen. The rule decides which subsets of working components count as successful
and therefore what the reported reliability means, yet it is rarely stated. Table~\ref{tab:lit_position}
compares this work with the closest studies.

\textbf{Contributions.} This paper poses the redundancy-allocation problem of a Wk/n:G system whose capacities
and demand are both triangular fuzzy numbers and solves it exactly. It makes three contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A declared comparison rule.} Capacity and demand are compared by a Hurwicz rule on $\alpha$-cuts
  with two parameters, a cut level $\alpha$ and an optimism index $\gamma$. The usual readings of imprecise data
  (the crisp model, the interval worst and best cases, and necessity-, possibility- and credibility-based chance
  constraints) are points of this policy square, so they can be compared on one instance with one solver.
  \item \textbf{The optimal design for every parameter value.} Because the system succeeds through the sum of
  its capacities, a common proportional spread scales that sum by one factor, and the two parameters act on the
  design only through one integer threshold. A number of crisp problems of the same order as a $30$-cell grid, one per threshold, gives the
  optimal design at every point of the square instead of at the points of a grid, together with the exact
  regions on which each design is optimal.
  \item \textbf{A certified exact solver and an engineering case study.} A two-phase branch-and-bound algorithm
  returns the most reliable of the minimum-cost designs and certifies its global optimality. On a generation
  fleet built from the IEEE Reliability Test System (RTS-79)\cite{IEEERTS1979}, the complete solution is a
  short menu of fleets with their cost and guarantee, and it shows what a grid study, the crisp model, one-sided
  fuzzy models and a probabilistic counterpart lose.
\end{enumerate}
The model and the rule are developed in Section~\ref{sec:theory}, the design problem and its exact solution in
Section~\ref{sec:redundancy_framework}, the case study in Section~\ref{sec:benchmarks} and the results in
Section~\ref{sec:results}; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses how a posture is chosen and the limitations.
Proofs, the notation table, two literature-derived instances that replicate the findings, the scaling study and
the script-level provenance of every number are given in the Supplementary Material.

\section{Dual-fuzzy model and comparison rule} \label{sec:theory}

\subsection{System model and $\alpha$-cuts}
The full notation is listed in Supplementary Material S11. Component $i\in\{1,\dots,n\}$ has state $s_i\in\{0,1\}$
and capacity $w_i>0$; for the set $S=\{i:s_i=1\}$ of working components the modal total capacity is
$W_S=\sum_{i\in S}w_i$, and the crisp system works if $W_S\ge k$. When capacities and demand are imprecise, the
dual-fuzzy model replaces them by triangular fuzzy numbers (TFNs) $\tilde{w}_i=(w_{i,L}^0,w_i,w_{i,U}^0)$ and
$\tilde{k}=(k_1,k_2,k_3)$. By Zadeh's extension principle the aggregate capacity of $S$ is
$\WS=\sum_{i\in S}\tilde{w}_i$, and the success condition becomes the comparison of two fuzzy quantities,
$\WS\,\tilde{\geq}\,\tilde{k}$. The $\alpha$-cut of a TFN $\tilde{A}=(a_1,a_2,a_3)$ at cut level $\alpha\in[0,1]$
is the interval
\begin{equation}
[A_L^\alpha, A_U^\alpha] = [a_1 + \alpha(a_2 - a_1),\; a_3 - \alpha(a_3 - a_2)],
\label{eq:tfn_cut}
\end{equation}
the closure of the support at $\alpha=0$; the cuts are nested and shrink to the modal value at $\alpha=1$.

\subsection{Hurwicz evaluation rule} \label{sec:hurwicz}
At cut level $\alpha$ the aggregate capacity of $S$ is the interval $[W_{S,L}^\alpha,W_{S,U}^\alpha]$ and the
threshold is $[k_L^\alpha,k_U^\alpha]$. When the two intervals overlap, the capacity is neither surely above nor
surely below the demand, and the success condition is undefined until a comparison rule is chosen. We compare
the Hurwicz values of the two intervals\cite{Hurwicz1951}, weighted by an optimism index $\gamma\in[0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
W_S^\alpha(\gamma) = \gamma W_{S,U}^\alpha + (1-\gamma) W_{S,L}^\alpha,
\label{eq:hurwicz_w}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\kag = k_U^\alpha - \gamma (k_U^\alpha - k_L^\alpha).
\label{eq:hurwicz_k}
\end{equation}

\textbf{\stmt{definition}{def:hurwicz}{Definition} (Hurwicz evaluation rule).}
For $\alpha,\gamma\in[0,1]$, the fuzzy success condition $\WS\,\tilde{\geq}\,\tilde{k}$ holds at $(\alpha,\gamma)$
if and only if
\begin{equation}
W_S^\alpha(\gamma) \geq \kag.
\label{eq:success}
\end{equation}

At $\gamma=0$ the rule compares the lowest capacity with the highest threshold (pessimistic evaluation); at
$\gamma=1$ the highest capacity with the lowest threshold (optimistic evaluation). The convex weighting of
interval endpoints is used for ranking fuzzy numbers under different risk attitudes\cite{CamposGonzalez1989,KimPark1990,LiouWang1992}.
The rule is defined on $\alpha$-cut intervals rather than on event spaces\cite{Yang2008}; Section~\ref{sec:family}
shows that its extreme columns nevertheless coincide with necessity- and possibility-based chance constraints.

\textbf{\stmt{lemma}{lem:crisp_equiv}{Lemma} (Crisp equivalence).} \textit{For any subset $S$, TFN capacities and
demand, and $\alpha,\gamma\in[0,1]$, let $w_i^\alpha(\gamma)=\gamma\,w_{i,U}^\alpha+(1-\gamma)\,w_{i,L}^\alpha$.
Then Equation~\eqref{eq:success} holds if and only if}
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i \in S} w_i^\alpha(\gamma) \;\geq\; \kag.
\label{eq:crisp_equiv}
\end{equation}

\textit{Proof.} The $\alpha$-cut endpoints of a sum of fuzzy numbers are the sums of the endpoints, so
$W_S^\alpha(\gamma)=\sum_{i\in S}[\gamma w_{i,U}^\alpha+(1-\gamma)w_{i,L}^\alpha]$. $\square$

For a fixed pair $(\alpha,\gamma)$, with common or unit-specific spreads, the fuzzy success condition is
therefore a crisp Wk/n:G condition with capacities $w_i^\alpha(\gamma)$ and threshold $\kag$, and dual fuzziness
defines a parameterized family of crisp Wk/n:G problems.

\subsection{Possibilistic readings of the rule} \label{sec:family}
For TFNs $\tilde{A}=(a_1,a_2,a_3)$ and $\tilde{B}=(b_1,b_2,b_3)$ the possibility and necessity of the event
$\tilde{A}\ge\tilde{B}$ are\cite{DuboisPrade1988}
\begin{equation}
\Pi(\tilde{A}\ge\tilde{B}) =
\begin{cases}
1, & a_2 \ge b_2,\\
\dfrac{a_3-b_1}{(a_3-a_2)+(b_2-b_1)}, & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
N(\tilde{A}\ge\tilde{B}) =
\begin{cases}
0, & a_2 \le b_2,\\
1-\dfrac{b_3-a_1}{(b_3-b_2)+(a_2-a_1)}, & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
both clipped to $[0,1]$. Possibility is the optimistic reading and necessity the pessimistic one; the
credibility measure $\mathrm{Cr}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\Pi+N)$ is self-dual\cite{Liu2002}. A decision maker may fix a
confidence level $\lambda$ instead of $(\alpha,\gamma)$ and require $N(\WS\ge\tilde{k})\ge\lambda$, a
possibilistic chance constraint\cite{InuiguchiRamik2000}. Proposition~\ref{prop:chance} maps such constraints
onto cells of the policy square; the proofs of the propositions and corollaries of this paper are given in
Supplementary Material S10.

\textbf{\stmt{proposition}{prop:chance}{Proposition} (Chance-constraint correspondence).}
\textit{Let the capacities $\tilde{w}_i$ and the threshold $\tilde{k}$ be TFNs, and let the spreads that govern each branch be non-degenerate: $\sum_{i \in S}(w_i - w_{i,L}^0) + (k_3 - k_2) > 0$ for the necessity direction and $\sum_{i \in S}(w_{i,U}^0 - w_i) + (k_2 - k_1) > 0$ for the possibility direction. Then, for every confidence level $\lambda \in (0,1]$,}
\begin{align}
N(\WS \ge \tilde{k}) \ge \lambda
&\iff
W_S^{\,\alpha}(\gamma) \ge \kag
\quad\text{at }(\alpha,\gamma) = (1-\lambda,\,0),\\
\Pi(\WS \ge \tilde{k}) \ge \lambda
&\iff
W_S^{\,\alpha}(\gamma) \ge \kag
\quad\text{at }(\alpha,\gamma) = (\lambda,\,1).
\end{align}

\textbf{\stmt{corollary}{cor:credibility}{Corollary} (Credibility chance constraints).} \textit{Under the
assumptions of Proposition~\ref{prop:chance}, for every $\lambda \in (0,1]$,
$\mathrm{Cr}(\WS \ge \tilde{k}) \ge \lambda$ holds if and only if Equation~\eqref{eq:success} holds at}
\begin{equation}
(\alpha,\gamma) =
\begin{cases}
(2\lambda,\,1), & \lambda \le 1/2,\\
(2-2\lambda,\,0), & \lambda > 1/2.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:credibility_cells}
\end{equation}

In the notation of the policy square, the equivalences restate the standard endpoint forms of necessity
and possibility constraints\cite{DuboisPrade1983,InuiguchiRamik2000,Iskander2005,Maity2011}: as $\alpha$ sweeps $[0,1]$, the column $\gamma=0$ enumerates every necessity-based chance
constraint and the column $\gamma=1$ every possibility-based one. A requirement such as ``demand met with
necessity at least $0.7$'' is the cell $(0.3,0)$. The interior columns interpolate between the two attitudes,
and with symmetric data the central column $\gamma=1/2$ is the modal comparison $W_S\ge k_2$, which is
also the credibility constraint $\mathrm{Cr}\ge 1/2$. Hurwicz evaluation
and possibilistic chance-constrained programming are therefore handled by one solver.

\subsection{Reliability under each rule} \label{sec:decomposition}
A reliability measure aggregates over subsets. For a design $\mathbf{x}$ (Section~\ref{sec:opt_model}) let
$I(\mathbf{x})$ be the set of subsystems in which a unit is installed, $p_i$ and $\tilde{w}_i$ the reliability
and capacity of the installed unit, and
$\Pr(S \mid \mathbf{x}) = \prod_{i \in S} p_i \prod_{l \in I(\mathbf{x}) \setminus S} (1-p_l)$. The static
reliability of $\mathbf{x}$ at $(\alpha,\gamma)$ is
\begin{equation}
\Rsys^{\alpha,\gamma}(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{S \subseteq I(\mathbf{x}),\; \sum_{i \in S} w_i^\alpha(\gamma) \ge \kag} \Pr(S \mid \mathbf{x}),
\label{eq:static}
\end{equation}
and the expected necessity and possibility reliabilities weight each subset by the necessity and the
possibility of its success:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
R_N(\mathbf{x}) &= \sum_{S \subseteq I(\mathbf{x})} \Pr(S \mid \mathbf{x}) \, N(\WS \ge \tilde{k}),\\
R_\Pi(\mathbf{x}) &= \sum_{S \subseteq I(\mathbf{x})} \Pr(S \mid \mathbf{x}) \, \Pi(\WS \ge \tilde{k}).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:rn_rpi_def}
\end{equation}

\textbf{\stmt{proposition}{prop:decomposition}{Proposition} (Column averages).} \textit{If the assumptions
of Proposition~\ref{prop:chance} hold for every subset $S\subseteq I(\mathbf{x})$ of a design $\mathbf{x}$, then}
\begin{equation}
R_{N}(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \int_0^1 \Rsys^{\alpha,0}(\mathbf{x})\,d\alpha,
\qquad
R_{\Pi}(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \int_0^1 \Rsys^{\alpha,1}(\mathbf{x})\,d\alpha .
\label{eq:decomposition}
\end{equation}

The expected necessity and possibility reliabilities are thus the averages of the pessimistic and optimistic
columns over the cut level. A single cell also has a chance-constrained reading: by
Proposition~\ref{prop:chance}, $\Rsys^{1-\lambda,0}(\mathbf{x}) \ge \Rtarget$ is equivalent to
$\Pr\{N(\WS \ge \tilde{k}) \ge \lambda\} \ge \Rtarget$, that is, with probability at least $\Rtarget$ the
available capacity clears the demand with necessity at least $\lambda$.

\textbf{\stmt{proposition}{prop:alpha_monotone}{Proposition} (Conditional monotonicity in $\alpha$).}
\textit{For a fixed $\gamma$, if $\frac{d}{d\alpha}W_S^\alpha(\gamma) \le 0$ and $\frac{d}{d\alpha}\kag \ge 0$ for
every subset $S$ of the installed components, the subsets accepted at $(\alpha,\gamma)$ are nested in $\alpha$
and $\Rsys^{1,\gamma} \leq \Rsys^{\alpha,\gamma} \leq \Rsys^{0,\gamma}$ for $\alpha\in[0,1]$.}

For symmetric spreads and a symmetric threshold the sign conditions hold for $\gamma\ge 1/2$; for $\gamma<1/2$,
which includes the necessity column, the ordering is reversed, and at $\gamma=1/2$ the reliability does not
depend on $\alpha$. For asymmetric data neither ordering need hold (Supplementary Material S10). The solver does
not rely on monotonicity; it only simplifies the interpretation of the results.

\section{Design problem and exact solution} \label{sec:redundancy_framework}

\subsection{Redundancy-allocation model} \label{sec:opt_model}
Each subsystem $i$ is assigned exactly one alternative $x_i$ from a discrete catalog $M_i$, which may contain a
null option (no unit installed). Option $j\in M_i$ has a fuzzy capacity $\tilde{w}_{i,j}$, a reliability
$p_{i,j}$ and a capital cost $c_{i,j}$. For a declared posture $(\alpha,\gamma)$ the problem is to minimize the
capital cost subject to the reliability target, and to break ties in favor of the most reliable design:
\begin{align}
\min_{\mathbf{x}} \quad & Z(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i(x_i) \label{eq:opt_cost} \\
\text{subject to} \quad & \Rsys^{\alpha,\gamma}(\mathbf{x}) \;\ge\; \Rtarget, \quad x_i \in M_i,\; i=1,\dots,n, \label{eq:opt_rel}
\end{align}
\begin{equation}
\max_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X}^*} \quad \Rsys^{\alpha,\gamma}(\mathbf{x}),
\label{eq:opt_tiebreak}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{X}^*$ is the set of minimum-cost feasible designs. When $\Rtarget$ is a contractual
specification, this lexicographic formulation uses only the problem data, whereas a fuzzy-goal aggregation
would add tolerance parameters\cite{Zimmermann2001}; it also admits a certificate of global optimality.

\subsection{Reduction of the policy square} \label{sec:reduction}
The dual-fuzzy formulation asks for one optimization per point of the square $[0,1]^2$, and computational
studies of fuzzy allocation typically report results on a coarse grid. Assume, as in the base case of
Section~\ref{sec:benchmarks}, that every capacity is fuzzified with the same proportional spreads,
$\tilde{w}_i = \big(w_i(1-\delta_l),\, w_i,\, w_i(1+\delta_r)\big)$, $\delta_l,\delta_r\in[0,1)$.

\textbf{\stmt{theorem}{thm:reduction}{Theorem} (Reduction to one threshold).}
\textit{Let the capacities be proportionally fuzzified with common spreads $\delta_l,\delta_r \in [0,1)$,
let the threshold $\tilde{k}$ be an arbitrary TFN, and let $\alpha,\gamma \in [0,1]$. With the
capacity multiplier}
\begin{equation}
m(\alpha,\gamma) \;=\; 1 - \delta_l(1-\alpha) + \gamma(\delta_l+\delta_r)(1-\alpha) \;>\;0,
\label{eq:multiplier}
\end{equation}
\textit{which does not depend on the subset $S$, the success condition of Equation~\eqref{eq:success} is
equivalent to a comparison of the modal total with one scalar,}
\begin{equation}
W_S^{\alpha}(\gamma) \ge \kag
\iff
W_S \;\ge\; \kappa(\alpha,\gamma) \;=\; \frac{\kag}{m(\alpha,\gamma)} .
\label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}

\textit{Proof.} Every capacity is a scalar multiple of one common unit, $\tilde{w}_i = w_i\tilde{u}$ with
$\tilde{u}=(1-\delta_l,1,1+\delta_r)$. Scalar multiplication distributes over the extension-principle
sum\cite{DuboisPrade1978}, so $\WS=W_S\tilde{u}$, $W_{S,L}^{\alpha}=W_S(1-\delta_l(1-\alpha))$ and
$W_{S,U}^{\alpha}=W_S(1+\delta_r(1-\alpha))$. Substituting into Equation~\eqref{eq:hurwicz_w} gives
$W_S^{\alpha}(\gamma)=W_S\,m(\alpha,\gamma)$, and $m>0$ because $\delta_l<1$. $\square$

The argument is the distributivity used in reductions of possibilistic constraints\cite{InuiguchiRamik2000,Rommelfanger1996,CadenasVerdegay1997};
what it buys here comes from the weighted structure. With integer modal capacities, the pair $(\alpha,\gamma)$
enters the design problem only through the integer $T=\lceil\kappa(\alpha,\gamma)\rceil$, so two postures with
the same $T$ have the same optimal design, and the square splits into finitely many regions with closed-form
boundaries. This extends parametric analysis\cite{CarlssonKorhonen1986} to discrete multi-choice catalogs.

\textbf{\stmt{corollary}{cor:partition}{Corollary} (Closed-form region boundaries).}
\textit{For each integer threshold $T$ and cut level $\alpha \in [0, 1)$, the curve separating $\kappa(\alpha,\gamma) \le T$
from $\kappa(\alpha,\gamma) > T$ is}
\begin{equation}
\gamma_T(\alpha) \;=\; \frac{k_U^\alpha - T\big(1 - \delta_l(1-\alpha)\big)}{(k_U^\alpha - k_L^\alpha) + T(\delta_l + \delta_r)(1-\alpha)},
\label{eq:gamma_T}
\end{equation}
\textit{and, since $\kappa$ decreases in $\gamma$ for $\alpha<1$, $\lceil\kappa\rceil = T$ on
$\{\gamma \in [0,1] : \gamma_T(\alpha) \le \gamma < \gamma_{T-1}(\alpha)\}$.}

The proof equates $\kappa=T$, which is linear in $\gamma$; at $\alpha=1$ all TFNs collapse to their modal values
and $\kappa(1,\gamma)=k_2$. Solving one crisp problem per attainable value of $T$ therefore yields the optimal
design at \emph{every} point of the square, including the points between the grid lines. The reduction also
shrinks the computation: the reliability recursion of Section~\ref{sec:solver} carries a state space
proportional to the integer threshold, and $T$ replaces a much larger threshold obtained by scaling the fuzzy
endpoints to integers. The reduction requires common proportional spreads; with component-specific spreads the
multiplier depends on the subset and each cell must be solved separately (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}).

\subsection{Two-phase exact solver} \label{sec:solver}
Metaheuristics dominate recent allocation studies\cite{DeviGarg2023}, but instances of moderate size remain
within reach of exact search\cite{Garg2013,Kuo2007}. The solver combines a depth-first search over the catalogs
with the capped-capacity convolution for weighted systems\cite{WuChen1994,DingZuoLisnianski2010,Bisht2023}.
A partial design with subsystems $1,\dots,i-1$ assigned is described by its cost $Z_{\mathrm{part}}$, its
capacity $W_{\mathrm{part}}$ and the probability mass function $\pi_{\mathrm{part}}$ of its capacity, capped at
the threshold; all capacities are the effective capacities $w^\alpha(\gamma)$ of the cell being solved. When
option $j$ is considered for subsystem $i$, $Z_{\mathrm{next}} = Z_{\mathrm{part}} + c_{i,j}$ and
$q_{\mathrm{next}} = \max\{0,\,\kag - W_{\mathrm{part}} - w_{i,j}\}$ is the capacity still required. Two suffix
quantities bound the completions: $W^{\mathrm{max}}_i = \sum_{l>i} w_l^{\mathrm{max}}$, and $\Zsuf_{i}(q)$, the
minimum additional cost of subsystems $i+1,\dots,n$ that supply at least $q$ further units of capacity,
computed once by a suffix dynamic program.

Solving both lexicographic levels in one traversal would require tracking equal-cost subtrees while the cost
incumbent is still decreasing. The search is therefore split into two exact phases. Phase~1 certifies the
minimum feasible cost $Z^*$; Phase~2 fixes $Z^*$ and searches only the designs of cost $Z^*$ for the most
reliable one. Both phases explore options in ascending cost order and apply two pruning tests: a subtree is
discarded if even the largest units of the remaining subsystems cannot reach the threshold or if its cost lower
bound $Z_{\mathrm{next}}+\Zsuf_i(q_{\mathrm{next}})$ \emph{strictly} exceeds the incumbent; and if an optimistic
completion, in which every unassigned subsystem carries the largest reliability and the largest effective
capacity of its catalog, falls below $\Rtarget$. The incumbent is seeded with the cost of a greedy feasible
design. Algorithm~\ref{alg:exact_solver} states the search. Capacity comparisons are exact, because the
$\alpha$-cut endpoints and Hurwicz values are computed in rational arithmetic and scaled to integers;
reliability comparisons use the tolerance $\epsilon = 10^{-12}$, so the optimality guarantee holds up to
$\epsilon$ on the reliability side.

\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{Two-phase dual-fuzzy exact solver. The state variables and suffix quantities are defined in
Section~\ref{sec:solver}; the implementation applies the pruning tests on entry to the child node.}
\label{alg:exact_solver}
\SetAlgoLined
\KwIn{Catalogs $M_1, \dots, M_n$, threshold $\tilde{k}$, target $\Rtarget$, cell $(\alpha,\gamma)$}
\KwOut{Lexicographically optimal design $\mathbf{x}^*$, certified minimum cost $Z^*$, reliability $R^*$}
\textbf{Phase 0:} compute $\kag$, $w_{i,j}^\alpha(\gamma)$, $W^{\mathrm{max}}_i$ and $\Zsuf_i(\cdot)$\;
\textbf{Phase 1:} initialize $Z^*$ with the cost of a greedy feasible design, or $\infty$\;
\SetKwFunction{FPhaseOne}{SearchPhase1}
\SetKwProg{Fn}{Function}{:}{}
\Fn{\FPhaseOne{$i, Z_{\mathrm{part}}, W_{\mathrm{part}}, \pi_{\mathrm{part}}$}}{
    \If{$i > n$}{
        \lIf{$\Rsys(\pi_{\mathrm{part}}) \ge \Rtarget$}{$Z^* \gets \min(Z^*, Z_{\mathrm{part}})$}
        \Return\;
    }
    \ForEach{option $j \in M_i$ sorted by ascending cost}{
        $Z_{\mathrm{next}} \gets Z_{\mathrm{part}} + c_{i,j}$;\quad
        $q_{\mathrm{next}} \gets \max\{0,\, \kag - W_{\mathrm{part}} - w_{i,j}\}$\;
        \lIf{$Z_{\mathrm{next}} + \Zsuf_{i}(q_{\mathrm{next}}) > Z^*$ \textbf{or} $W_{\mathrm{part}} + w_{i,j} + W^{\mathrm{max}}_i < \kag$}{\textbf{continue}}
        $\pi_{\mathrm{next}} \gets \texttt{Convolve}(\pi_{\mathrm{part}}, p_{i,j}, w_{i,j})$\;
        \lIf{$\texttt{OptimisticReliability}(\pi_{\mathrm{next}}, i) < \Rtarget$}{\textbf{continue}}
        \FPhaseOne{$i+1, Z_{\mathrm{next}}, W_{\mathrm{part}} + w_{i,j}, \pi_{\mathrm{next}}$}\;
    }
}
\FPhaseOne{$1, 0, 0, \pi_0$}\;
\lIf{$Z^* = \infty$}{\Return \textsf{Infeasible}}
\textbf{Phase 2:} repeat the traversal with $Z^*$ fixed and the same pruning tests, carrying the partial design; at each leaf with $Z_{\mathrm{part}} = Z^*$ and $\Rsys(\pi_{\mathrm{part}}) > R^*$ set $R^* \gets \Rsys(\pi_{\mathrm{part}})$, $\mathbf{x}^* \gets \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{part}}$\;
\Return $(\mathbf{x}^*, Z^*, R^*)$\;
\end{algorithm}

Global optimality requires the pruning tests to be admissible. The proofs of the next three results are
given in Supplementary Material S10.

\textbf{\stmt{theorem}{thm:bb_bounds}{Theorem} (Residual-capacity and suffix-cost bounds).}
\textit{Let capacities and costs be non-negative, $\Rtarget>0$, and $Z^*$ the incumbent cost. (i) If $W_{\mathrm{part}} + w_{i,j} + W^{\mathrm{max}}_i < \kag$,
every completion has zero reliability. (ii) If $Z_{\mathrm{next}} + \Zsuf_{i}(q_{\mathrm{next}}) > Z^{*}$, no
completion improves on the incumbent under the lexicographic objective.}

The inequality in (ii) must be strict: a completion of equal cost may still improve the secondary objective,
which is why Algorithm~\ref{alg:exact_solver} tests $>$ and not $\ge$.

\textbf{\stmt{theorem}{thm:rel_bound}{Theorem} (Optimistic reliability bound).}
\textit{Let $R_{\mathrm{ub}}$ be the reliability obtained by convolving the capped capacity distribution of a
partial design with, for each unassigned subsystem, a virtual option carrying the largest reliability and the
largest effective capacity of its catalog. No completion has reliability greater than $R_{\mathrm{ub}}$.}

\textbf{\stmt{theorem}{thm:optimality}{Theorem} (Termination and global lexicographic optimality).}
\textit{For finite $n$ and catalogs, Algorithm~\ref{alg:exact_solver} terminates, and at termination
$(\mathbf{x}^*, Z^*, R^*)$ solves Equations~\eqref{eq:opt_cost}--\eqref{eq:opt_tiebreak}, or the algorithm
correctly declares that no design meets the target.}

A solution returned by Algorithm~\ref{alg:exact_solver} is called \emph{certified}. A numerical trace of both
phases on a three-component system is given in Supplementary Material S1. The search space has $O(|M|^n)$
designs and evaluating one design costs $O(n\cdot T)$ with the capped convolution; the bounds do not remove the
exponential worst case, and their effect in practice is reported in Section~\ref{sec:scaling}.

\subsection{Cost--necessity frontier} \label{sec:frontier_model}
Each cell minimizes cost subject to a reliability target at one effective threshold, and the expected
necessity reliability $R_N$ does not enter that optimization. A decision maker who prices necessity directly
needs a different problem: for each required level $r$,
\begin{equation}
\min_{\mathbf{x}} Z(\mathbf{x}) \quad \text{subject to} \quad R_N(\mathbf{x}) \ge r,
\label{eq:biobjective_pareto}
\end{equation}
and, when the design must also meet the target at the modal demand, with $R_{\mathrm{modal}}(\mathbf{x})$ the
crisp reliability at $k_2$,
\begin{equation}
\min_{\mathbf{x}} Z(\mathbf{x}) \quad \text{subject to} \quad R_N(\mathbf{x}) \ge r, \quad R_{\mathrm{modal}}(\mathbf{x}) \ge \Rtarget.
\label{eq:biobjective_pareto_adequate}
\end{equation}
By Equation~\eqref{eq:decomposition}, $R_N$ averages over many thresholds, so the single-threshold bounds do not
apply; the frontier is computed here by exhaustive enumeration.

\section{Case study: IEEE RTS-79 generation fleet} \label{sec:benchmarks}

\subsection{Instance and protocol} \label{sec:rts_benchmark}
The generation fleet of the IEEE Reliability Test System\cite{IEEERTS1979} has $n=10$ bays that must meet a
peak regional demand $\tilde{k}=(162,180,198)$~MW, whose spread represents the uncertainty of the peak-load
forecast, with the reliability target $\Rtarget=0.95$. Each bay offers a null option and two of the six RTS-79
unit classes of Table~\ref{tab:rts_units}, so the catalog has $3^{10}=59{,}049$ designs; hydro units are
available only at Bays~3--6, so a fleet contains at most four of them. The availability of a unit is
$p=1-\mathrm{FOR}=\mathrm{MTTF}/(\mathrm{MTTF}+\mathrm{MTTR})$, with the forced outage rate (FOR) and the mean
times to failure and to repair computed from the unit statistics of the source. RTS-79 reports operating costs
only, so the capital costs are an illustrative assumption of this study. The static reliability of a fleet is
the probability of no capacity shortfall, computed from the capacity outage probability distribution of its
units; for the crisp model at the modal demand, $\Rsys\ge 0.95$ is a single-snapshot loss-of-load probability of
at most $0.05$ at peak demand. The complete catalog is listed in Supplementary Material S6.

\textbf{Capacity spreads.} In the base case every capacity is a TFN with the common proportional spread
$\delta=0.10$, $\tilde{w}_i=(0.9w_i,\,w_i,\,1.1w_i)$. The spread has an operational motivation: the output of
thermal units, and of combustion turbines in particular, falls in hot weather as combustion air density drops,
whereas hydraulic output is largely insensitive to air temperature. RTS-79 gives seasonal capacity limits for its
hydro units but no partial-outage data for thermal units, so the base case uses one spread for all units, and
Section~\ref{sec:robustness} replaces it by unit-specific spreads.

\textbf{Protocol.} The instance is solved on the $30$-cell grid $\alpha\in\{0.0,\allowbreak 0.3,\allowbreak 0.5,\allowbreak 0.7,\allowbreak 0.9,\allowbreak 1.0\}$,
$\gamma\in\{0.0,\allowbreak 0.3,\allowbreak 0.5,\allowbreak 0.7,\allowbreak 1.0\}$, and the complete parametric
solution of Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction} is computed over the whole square. Two instances derived from published
allocation problems, a $14$-subsystem instance based on Fyffe et al.\cite{Fyffe1968} and a $10$-subsystem
instance based on the pharmaceutical plant of Garg and Sharma\cite{Garg2013}, are used to check whether the
findings recur; neither is the original optimization problem, and their catalogs are given in
Supplementary Material S4 and S5, and the replication of the findings in Supplementary Material S8. A synthetic family measures scalability (Section~\ref{sec:scaling}). The
computational pipeline and the script behind each result are documented in Supplementary Material S2.

\subsection{Baselines} \label{sec:baselines_def}
The complete parametric solution is compared with three kinds of baseline.

\emph{Grid.} On the region of the square whose optimum is at no cell of the $30$-cell grid we measure how often
the design of the nearest grid cell fails the reliability target, and how much more than the optimum the
cheapest grid design that meets it costs.

\emph{Simpler models with the same data.} The crisp model uses the modal capacities and demand, so its
threshold is $\lceil k_2\rceil$; the model with fuzzy capacities only has threshold $\lceil k_2/m(\alpha,\gamma)\rceil$;
the model with fuzzy demand only has threshold $\lceil \kag\rceil$. Each baseline design is the optimum at its
own threshold and is evaluated at the threshold of the dual-fuzzy model at the same posture: it either fails
the target or meets it at a cost no lower than the optimum. The probabilistic counterpart treats the same
imprecision as randomness: a common derating factor $\Theta\in[1-\delta_l,\,1+\delta_r]$ and the demand
$D\in[k_1,k_3]$ are independent random variables with densities on the supports of the fuzzy data, success is
$\Theta W_S\ge D$, that is $W_S\ge\hat{T}=\lceil D/\Theta\rceil$, and
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{prob}}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}\big[R(\mathbf{x},\hat{T})\big] = \sum_{T} \Pr\{\hat{T} = T\}\, R(\mathbf{x},T),
\label{eq:rprob}
\end{equation}
where $R(\mathbf{x},T)$ is the crisp reliability at the integer threshold $T$. The probabilistic design
minimizes cost subject to $R_{\mathrm{prob}}(\mathbf{x}) \ge \Rtarget$; we read the membership functions as
triangular densities and repeat the computation with uniform densities. Because $R_{\mathrm{prob}}$ mixes many
thresholds, it is solved by enumeration.

\emph{Algorithm.} A genetic algorithm with matched computational effort (Section~\ref{sec:scaling}).

The reference of the model baselines is the declared dual-fuzzy rule: the comparison measures what a planner
who regards both sides as imprecise, and has declared a posture, loses by solving a simpler model; it does not
show that the simpler models are wrong for a planner who accepts their assumptions. By
Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction} a one-sided model with a recalibrated spread can reproduce the dual threshold in any
one cell; the comparison is made at the same data, in which the capacity and demand spreads are separate inputs.
All areas are integrals over the square on the closed-form regions of Corollary~\ref{cor:partition}, computed by
the midpoint rule in $\alpha$, and shares are of the part of the square on which some design meets the target
(Supplementary Material S7).

\subsection{Menu designs need not be efficient in necessity} \label{sec:frontier}
Each menu design minimizes cost subject to the target in its own cell; it is not selected for its necessity
reliability. We enumerated all $3^{10} = 59{,}049$ designs and solved the frontier problems of
Section~\ref{sec:frontier_model} (Figure~\ref{fig:true_pareto_frontier}). Without the adequacy constraint the
frontier contains designs cheaper than \$180\,M with positive necessity, such as the \$170\,M fleet of two
100~MW oil units, whose crisp adequacy is only $0.960^2 = 0.9216$. With the adequacy constraint no design
cheaper than \$180\,M is admissible, and between D6 and D7 the frontier contains three designs that no Hurwicz
cell prescribes: a planner bound by adequacy obtains $R_N = 0.6392$ for \$190\,M, $R_N = 0.8804$ for \$194\,M and
$R_N = 0.9171$ for \$198\,M, against $0.9509$ for D7 at \$203\,M. The menu and the frontier differ because each
menu design is the cheapest design at a single effective threshold, and $R_N$ does not enter that
optimization. Supplementary Material S6 lists the frontier designs and the dominated catalog fleets.

\subsection{Robustness to the spread model and the target} \label{sec:robustness}
The capacity spread, the demand spread and the target are assumptions rather than data, and
Supplementary Material S6 varies each of them on the fleet. The main results are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Size of the common spread.} For $\delta$ from $0.02$ to $0.20$ the necessity-cell threshold
$T=\lceil 198/(1-\delta)\rceil$ rises from $203$ to $248$~MW and the cost from \$180\,M to \$265\,M
($+47.2\%$); the modal cell stays at $T=180$~MW and \$180\,M for every $\delta$, because $m(\alpha,1/2)=1$ for
symmetric spreads; in the optimistic boundary cell the cost falls to \$127\,M at $\delta=0.20$, with zero
necessity of meeting demand.
\item \emph{Asymmetric spreads.} Seasonal regimes such as summer, $(\delta_l,\delta_r)=(0.15,0.05)$, raise the
necessity-cell cost to \$231\,M at $T=233$~MW. Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction} allows $\delta_l\ne\delta_r$, so
every regime is solved with the same certificate.
\item \emph{Demand forecast.} Widening the demand half-width from $0.02$ to $0.20$ raises the necessity-cell cost
from \$180\,M to \$256\,M and lowers the optimistic boundary cost to \$127\,M.
\item \emph{Reliability target.} At $\Rtarget=0.95$ the cost-minimizing fleets of the three cells are series
arrangements at their threshold: the loss of any single unit drops the working capacity below $T$. Over
$\Rtarget\in\{0.90,\dots,0.99\}$, a fleet that still reaches $T$ after the loss of its largest unit becomes
optimal at $\Rtarget=0.97$ in the modal cell (\$212\,M) and at $0.98$ in the other two (\$180\,M and \$266\,M).
\item \emph{Unit-specific spreads.} With the hydro capacity $(45,50,50)$~MW given by the seasonal limits of
RTS-79 and assumed thermal spreads, the reduction fails and each of the $30$ cells is solved by the unreduced
exact search (all within $600$~s). The optimal fleet changes in $7$ of the $30$ cells and its cost in $6$, in
both directions: on the necessity side the common spread over-invests (at $(0.3,0)$ the unit-specific optimum
costs \$180\,M instead of \$203\,M), and on the possibility side it under-invests by \$9--22\,M in five cells.
Under perturbed thermal spreads one cell selects a \$171\,M fleet (one 155~MW coal unit and two 20~MW
combustion turbines) that is the crisp optimum of no integer threshold, so heterogeneous spreads can select a design that only the unreduced search finds.
\end{itemize}
Across these variants the declared posture keeps changing the prescribed fleet and its cost, in both
directions.

\subsection{Computational performance} \label{sec:scaling} \label{sec:baseline}
On a synthetic family with three choices per subsystem, $n\in\{8,10,\dots,20\}$ and three demand profiles,
all $21$ instances were certified within a $600$~s budget; the slowest, $n=20$ under the design profile, took
$459.24$~s and evaluated the exact reliability of only $89$ of $3.49\times10^{9}$ complete designs. Re-solving the $n=14$ instance without the
reduction, against a threshold obtained by scaling the fuzzy endpoints to integers ($16{,}767$ against $T=66$),
took $60.69$~s against $0.268$~s, a factor of $226.8$, close to the ratio of the thresholds; the reduced and
unreduced formulations agreed on all $12$ instances up to $n=14$. A genetic algorithm given the same computing
budget and an equal-cost local refinement reached the minimum cost on all $17$ instances tested, but at $n=20$
some seeds settled on a design of the same cost whose reliability is $0.9035$ against the certified $0.9112$.
Without a certificate, nothing in the heuristic's output indicates that a more reliable design exists at the
same cost. Supplementary Material S9 gives the scaling table, the ablation and the genetic-algorithm protocol.

\section{Discussion} \label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Choosing the posture} \label{sec:managerial}
The square divides into three zones. Low $\alpha$ with low $\gamma$ compares the worst-case capacity with the
worst-case threshold on the widest cut; by Proposition~\ref{prop:chance} the cell $(\alpha,0)$ demands necessity
$1-\alpha$. This zone gives the highest cost (\$203\,M on the fleet) and suits safety-critical infrastructure in
which shortfalls carry severe penalties. Low $\alpha$ with high $\gamma$ approaches the optimistic boundary cell,
with the lowest cost (\$130\,M) and zero necessity of meeting demand; it suits non-critical deployments in which
occasional shortfalls are tolerable. As $\alpha\to1$ all columns converge on the crisp design (\$180\,M).
The pair $(\alpha,\gamma)$ need not be chosen as two free numbers; there are three ways of fixing it, in
decreasing order of what they require from the decision maker.

\textbf{(i) A prescribed necessity level.} When a regulation, a contract or an internal standard requires demand
to be met with necessity at least $\lambda$, Proposition~\ref{prop:chance} fixes the cell $(1-\lambda,0)$, and no
optimism index has to be elicited. A possibility or credibility requirement fixes a cell in the same way
(Table~\ref{tab:nesting}).

\textbf{(ii) A design menu.} Without such a requirement, the decision maker can choose among designs instead of
parameters. The complete parametric solution is a finite menu: on the RTS-79 fleet the seven designs of
Table~\ref{tab:policy_menu}, each with its cost, its necessity and possibility reliabilities and the share of
the square on which it is optimal. A capital budget $\bar{Z}$ is a special case: the menu gives the design with
the largest necessity reliability among those costing at most $\bar{Z}$. With $\bar{Z}$ set to \$150\,M no
affordable design has a positive necessity of meeting demand, \$180\,M buys $R_N = 0.617$ (D6, the better of the
two \$180\,M designs) and \$203\,M buys $R_N = 0.951$; under crisp adequacy the catalog offers $R_N = 0.880$ for
\$194\,M and $0.917$ for \$198\,M (Section~\ref{sec:frontier}), designs that are not on the menu.

\textbf{(iii) An elicited optimism index.} If an interior $\gamma$ is wanted, it follows from
Equation~\eqref{eq:hurwicz_w} once the decision maker states a certainty equivalent $C^*$ for an interval
$[C_{\min},C_{\max}]$: $\gamma = (C^*-C_{\min})/(C_{\max}-C_{\min})$. Because the optimal design is piecewise
constant in $\gamma$, the elicited value matters only through the region it falls in, which
Corollary~\ref{cor:partition} gives exactly. At $\alpha=0.5$, for example, the modal design D5 is optimal for
every $\gamma<0.612$, so an elicitation error below that value does not change the prescription; at $\alpha=0$
the necessity design D7 is optimal only for $\gamma<0.175$. The attitude of an individual manager is a weaker
basis for safety-critical infrastructure than a prescribed necessity level, and we regard (iii) as a fallback.

\textbf{What the baselines imply.} A planner who solves the crisp model, or fuzzifies one side only, commits
without stating it to a design that is optimal for some postures and not for others, and Section~\ref{sec:baselines_res}
shows how large the part of the square is on which that design fails the target. The probabilistic counterpart
is not a neutral alternative: it needs densities that expert-based data do not fix, and on the case study its
prescription changed with the density. Declaring the posture and solving over the whole square makes the
commitment explicit and its consequences measurable. Possibilistic evaluation reduces to comparisons of
$\alpha$-cut endpoints, which is what allows the exact solver to propagate capped capacity distributions; Liu's
uncertainty theory\cite{Liu2015} is a different formalism, and replacing the possibilistic oracle by the
uncertain-measure oracle of Wei et al.\cite{WeiShiZhang2025a} changes the prescription on both derived
instances (Supplementary Material S4; the oracle is verified in Supplementary Material S3).

\subsection{Limitations} \label{sec:limitations}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Certified solution is demonstrated up to $n=20$ with three choices per subsystem; larger systems may require
decomposition or hybridization with heuristics.
\item The reduction of Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction} requires capacity spreads that are proportional and common to
all components ($\delta_l\ne\delta_r$ is allowed). With component-specific spreads the exact search still
applies cell by cell, at a higher computational cost, and the crisp family of designs need not contain the
optimum.
\item The cost--necessity frontier and the probabilistic counterpart average over many thresholds, so they were
computed by exhaustive enumeration, which limits them to catalogs of moderate size. Robust counterparts, such as
budgeted uncertainty\cite{BertsimasSim2004} or minimax regret\cite{FeizollahiModarres2012}, are not points of the
square and were not computed.
\item Components are statistically independent; shared-load and cascading-failure dependencies\cite{WU2025110678}
are not modeled.
\item The capital costs of the case study are an assumption, the two derived instances are adaptations rather
than the original problems, and the results depend on the shapes of the membership functions, which should be
elicited and reviewed with domain experts before industrial use.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}
When the component capacities and the demand threshold of a weighted $k$-out-of-$n$:G system are both imprecise,
the success condition needs a comparison rule. This paper declares that rule as a Hurwicz comparison on
$\alpha$-cuts, which contains the crisp model, the interval worst and best cases and the necessity-, possibility-
and credibility-based chance constraints as points of one policy square. Under common proportional capacity
spreads the square acts on the design problem through one effective integer threshold, so the optimal design is
known at every point of the square from a number of crisp problems of the same order as a $30$-cell grid, each certified by a two-phase
branch-and-bound algorithm. On a generation fleet built from the IEEE Reliability Test System the complete
solution is a menu of seven fleets costing \$130--203\,M; a $30$-cell grid over the indices never finds two of them, including a
\$180\,M fleet with a higher necessity of meeting demand than the crisp-optimal fleet of the same cost, and the
crisp design fails the target or overpays on a large part of the square. Simpler models of the imprecision
thus commit the planner to an undeclared posture, whereas the complete solution prices each posture and lets the
planner choose a design. Extending the reduction to component-specific spreads, an exact method for the
cost--necessity frontier and dependence between components are the main directions for further work.
